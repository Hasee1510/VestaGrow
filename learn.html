What is a SIP and how does it work?

A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) lets you invest a fixed amount in a mutual fund at regular intervals, usually every month, instead of investing a large sum at once.

Each instalment buys units at that day's NAV (net asset value). When the NAV is low you receive more units, and when it is high you receive fewer. Over time this can bring your average cost per unit closer to the middle of the price range. This is called rupee cost averaging.

A simple illustration

Suppose you invest ₹5,000 each month for three months, and the NAV is ₹100, ₹80 and ₹125:

  • Month 1: 50 units
  • Month 2: 62.5 units
  • Month 3: 40 units

Your ₹15,000 buys 152.5 units, an average cost of about ₹98.36 per unit, while the average NAV was about ₹101.67. This example is illustrative only and is not a forecast.

Before you start

A SIP builds investing discipline, but it does not guarantee profit or protect against loss. Decide your goal, your time horizon and an amount you can keep up comfortably, then choose a fund category that matches your risk comfort.

Build an emergency fund first

Before you invest, set aside money for the unexpected, such as a job loss or a medical bill. Without it, you may be forced to sell investments at a bad time or take an expensive loan.

How much?

A common rule of thumb is three to six months of essential expenses: rent or EMIs, food, utilities, insurance premiums and school fees. Aim for more if your income is irregular or you have dependants.

Where to keep it

Keep it somewhere safe and easy to reach, such as a savings account or similar low-risk option. Access matters more than returns here.

How to build it

  • Add up your monthly essential expenses and multiply by your target number of months.
  • Set up an automatic monthly transfer until you reach that amount.
  • Refill the fund whenever you use it.

Understanding risk and diversification

Every investment carries some risk. The goal is not to avoid risk completely, but to take a level of risk that suits you.

Risk tolerance and capacity

Risk tolerance is how comfortable you are with your investments rising and falling. Risk capacity is how much loss you can actually absorb, which depends on your income, dependants and goals. Both matter.

Time horizon

Money you need in one or two years should not sit in highly volatile investments. Longer goals, such as retirement, can usually ride out more ups and downs.

Diversification

Spreading your money across asset classes (such as equity, debt and gold) and across sectors means no single outcome can sink your whole portfolio. Review and rebalance at least once a year.

Diversification does not guarantee a profit or protect against loss in a declining market.
